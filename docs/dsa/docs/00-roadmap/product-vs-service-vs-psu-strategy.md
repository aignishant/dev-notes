# Product vs Service vs PSU — the three interview cultures

> Three different worlds. Same job title (software engineer). Three different hiring philosophies. Three different prep playbooks.

If you don't pick one and prep for it, you'll be average at all three. Pick one as primary; the others come for free if your primary is harder.

---

## The three worlds at a glance

| | **Product** | **Service** | **PSU** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What they sell** | Their own product (Google Search, Amazon Store, Uber app) | Software services to other companies (build-a-bank-app, run-this-system) | Government / strategic missions (rockets, defense, nuclear) |
| **Examples** | Google, Meta, Amazon, Stripe, Flipkart | TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Cognizant, Accenture | ISRO, DRDO, BARC, BEL, BHEL |
| **Hiring philosophy** | "Hire the best, pay them well, retain by interesting work" | "Hire competent, train on the job, scale by volume" | "Hire mission-aligned, retain by stability + meaning" |
| **Salary** | High (₹15–60L+ entry; ₹50L–4Cr+ senior) | Medium (₹3–10L entry; ₹15–40L senior) | Low-Medium (₹8–14L entry; capped) |
| **Pace** | Fast, results-driven | Project-driven, client-facing | Slow, methodical, mission-driven |
| **What they look for** | Problem-solving + design + culture fit | Communication + basics + adaptability | Theory + reliability + mission alignment |

## Product companies — the "hard problem" culture

### Their hiring philosophy

> "Most of our work is hard. We can train you on our codebase, but we can't train you on first-principles problem solving. So we hire for that."

They optimize for **upper bound** — they'd rather miss 5 mediocre candidates than hire 1. That's why they ask LeetCode-hard problems.

### What they test

- **5–7 rounds in a day** (or spread over a week)
- **3–4 coding rounds** (LeetCode medium-hard)
- **1–2 system design rounds** (senior+)
- **1–2 behavioral rounds** (culture, collaboration, leadership)
- **Sometimes:** team match round, hiring committee, executive review

### What "good" looks like

- You can solve 70%+ of LeetCode mediums in <30 minutes, on a clean whiteboard
- You can design a system serving 100M users + handle a "what breaks at 1B" follow-up
- You have 5–10 well-prepared behavioral stories
- You communicate while you code — interviewers value clarity > raw correctness

### Resume style

- **Quantified impact.** "Led migration that reduced p99 latency by 40%, saving $2M/yr."
- Senior-leader review-style bullets, not laundry lists
- Show production-grade ownership (designed, shipped, owned)

### How to position yourself

- Frame past work as **product impact**, not just task completion
- Learn the company's product deeply (use it, study it, mention it in answers)
- Know the company's recent engineering blogs (you'll get bonus points for referencing them in design rounds)

### Prep playbook

- Use this bible's **3-month fast-track** plan minimum (6-week thorough if pressed)
- Heavy emphasis on:
    - All 20 [Patterns](../04-patterns/index.md) mastered
    - 2–3 [System design](../08-system-design/index.md) Tier-1 projects
    - 8–10 STAR behavioral stories
- Mock interviews matter a lot — book them on Pramp or interviewing.io

### Common pitfalls

- ❌ "I'll learn the company's product the day before the interview." → Won't help.
- ❌ "I'll solve LeetCode hards directly." → You'll fail mediums.
- ❌ Memorizing solutions. → They smell it instantly.
- ❌ Not testing your code. → Interviewers note it.

## Service companies — the "communicator" culture

### Their hiring philosophy

> "We need 10,000 engineers per year, who can be deployed to client projects. We can train them on the project tech stack. We need them to be reliable, communicate clearly, and learn fast."

They optimize for **lower bound** — predictable competence, not exceptional talent.

### What they test

- **Aptitude round** (separate online assessment) — math, logic, English
- **1 coding round** — easy to medium (reverse string, basic DSA)
- **Technical Q&A** — CS fundamentals (OOP, DBMS, OS, CN, basic algorithms theory)
- **HR round** — "Why us?", communication, attitude, willingness to relocate

### What "good" looks like

- You can write working code for an easy/medium problem in 20 minutes
- You can clearly explain CS fundamentals (e.g., "What's a deadlock? How do you avoid it?")
- You communicate well in English, smile during the interview, show enthusiasm
- You're willing to relocate, work in any tech stack, and stay 2+ years

### Resume style

- **List-based.** Skills, courses, projects, internships.
- Highlight **breadth** (Java, Python, SQL, web, cloud basics).
- Mention **certifications** (AWS Cloud Practitioner, Google Cloud Associate) — they help.

### How to position yourself

- Frame yourself as **adaptable and willing to learn**
- Mention you're **open to any tech stack and any city**
- Have a **clean elevator pitch** (60-second "Tell me about yourself")

### Prep playbook

- Use this bible's **5-week balanced** plan
- Light on advanced topics; heavy on:
    - All 20 patterns at medium level
    - CS fundamentals (Phase 14 of this bible)
    - HR round prep
- Aptitude practice on IndiaBix, PrepInsta

### Common pitfalls

- ❌ Showing off advanced topics they don't ask about. Wastes interviewer time.
- ❌ Saying "I'm only interested in product companies." Nope, even if true, never say it.
- ❌ Being too informal. Service companies value formal etiquette more.
- ❌ Not having a clear "Why us?" answer.

## PSU companies — the "mission" culture

### Their hiring philosophy

> "We're building things that matter to the country (rockets, defense, energy). We want people who'll stay 30 years, take pride in the mission, and can work in panel-style hierarchy. Salary is fixed; meaning is the perk."

They optimize for **mission-fit + reliability + theory mastery**.

### What they test

- **Written exam** (GATE-level or organization-specific) — CS fundamentals + DSA
- **Panel interview** — 4–6 senior scientists
- **Theory-heavy** — OS, DBMS, Computer Networks, Compilers, Architecture
- **Some basic DSA** — often pseudocode rather than running code
- **Final-year project deep-dive** — be ready for 30 minutes on it
- **Behavioral / motivation** — "Why ISRO/DRDO?" matters more than at any other company

### What "good" looks like

- You score top quartile on the written exam
- You can answer "Explain virtual memory" with depth, not just keywords
- You can discuss your final-year project at researcher depth
- You communicate **respectfully** in panel format (one speaker at a time, formal)
- You have a **convincing mission-alignment story**

### Resume style

- **Project-heavy + academic-heavy.** Final-year project front and center.
- **Publications, patents, achievements** — list them.
- Conservative, formal, no flashy design.

### How to position yourself

- "I want to contribute to [ISRO's missions / DRDO's defense projects / BARC's nuclear program]"
- Mention any past involvement with national tech (hackathons, government internships, ISRO student programs)
- Show long-term commitment — PSU jobs are careers, not stepping stones, and they evaluate for that

### Prep playbook

- Use this bible's **6-week thorough** plan (slightly modified)
- Light on advanced DSA; **heavy on**:
    - CS fundamentals (Phase 14 of this bible) — OS, DBMS, CN, Compilers, Architecture
    - Final-year project — write a 1-page summary, rehearse 30-min deep-dive
    - "Why this PSU?" — write a 90-second answer, memorize, deliver naturally
- GATE-level practice problems are the gold standard for written exam

### Common pitfalls

- ❌ Treating it as a fallback to "real" companies. Panel sees through it.
- ❌ Underestimating CS theory. PSU panels go deep on basics.
- ❌ Being informal in the panel interview.
- ❌ Not having a long-term mission story.

## Which to pick

> 🎯 **Pick based on what you actually want, not what your peers say is "best."**

### Pick **product** if you:

- Get energized by hard, ambiguous problems
- Want high salary + fast career growth
- Are comfortable with high-stress interviews
- Want to work on cutting-edge tech
- Are OK with intense work culture

### Pick **service** if you:

- Want to ramp into industry comfortably
- Value variety — different clients, different tech, different cities
- Have strong communication but maybe weaker on raw problem-solving (yet)
- Plan to use it as a launchpad to product (very common path)
- Have campus placements as your main route

### Pick **PSU** if you:

- Care deeply about mission / national contribution
- Value job stability above salary
- Like methodical, rigorous engineering
- Are comfortable with hierarchy and panel culture
- Plan a 20–30 year career at one place

## The "I'll prep for all three" trap

You can — but understand the cost:

- **Product** prep is the deepest. If you're product-ready, you're service-ready and PSU-DSA-ready.
- **PSU** prep adds CS theory that product/service barely test. So PSU = product + theory.
- **Service** prep is the shallowest of the three.

So:

| Primary target | Effort to add other two |
|---|---|
| Product | +20% effort to add PSU theory; service is almost free |
| PSU | +50% effort to add product depth |
| Service | +200% effort to add product, +50% to add PSU theory |

If you're interviewing at all three, **prep primarily for product**. The CS-theory layer for PSU is a few extra hours per week.

## Switching tracks

### Service → Product

Common, doable. Steps:
1. Stay 1.5–2 years at the service company (signals stability)
2. Pick up product-style work on the side (open source, side projects, hackathons)
3. Grind LeetCode + system design seriously for 4–6 months
4. Apply to product companies, mentioning your service background as a strength (production experience)

### Product → PSU

Less common (mostly people leave PSU for product, not the other way). If you do it, frame as "wanting mission impact."

### PSU → Product

Common in mid-career. Frame your PSU work as **deep technical work, not just government job**. Highlight published papers, complex systems built. Product companies respect PSU technical talent — you just need to translate it.
