# Tips for Live Mocks (Pramp / interviewing.io)

> The bible can teach concepts and problems. The *conversational rhythm* of an interview only develops with another human across the table. This page is the operating manual for getting the most out of live mock platforms.

<span class="phase-status phase-done">Phase 14 — Mock Interview</span>

---

## Why live mocks matter

Reading a transcript and *being* in a transcript are different problems. Live mocks expose the things text can't:

- The 5-second silence after you say "let me think" — does it feel natural or does panic show in your voice?
- Whether your face freezes when you type a bug.
- How you respond to an interviewer who interrupts you mid-sentence.
- Whether you can recover when your first idea was wrong and you said it confidently.

Books and transcripts get you to ~70% of an offer. The last 30% is conversational reps.

## Pramp specifically

### How it works

You sign up for a slot, get matched with another candidate, each takes one 30-minute coding round (one problem). One person interviews; one solves. Then swap.

### Getting good rounds on Pramp

Pramp pairs you randomly. Half your matches will be excellent; half will be at a level below you. Here's how to extract value either way:

- **You get a strong partner**: do the problem like a real interview. Take the feedback seriously.
- **You get a weak partner**: take the *interviewer* seat as practice. Coaching someone else through a problem teaches you what good interview hygiene looks like from the other side.
- **You get a no-show**: most platforms let you re-queue immediately.

### Hidden value: being the interviewer

This is underrated. When you interview someone:

- You see what panicked silence looks like from the outside. Then you know what *you* look like.
- You learn what "good" coding looks like when it's not your code. Style and naming matter more than you think.
- You hear your own voice asking clarifying questions and realise where you ramble.

Take Pramp's interviewer side as seriously as the candidate side.

## interviewing.io specifically

### How it works

Anonymous voice-only interview with a real engineer (employed at a FAANG / unicorn). They don't see your face, name, or resume until the end. You can opt to reveal yourself; if the interviewer is at a company you'd join, they can refer you internally.

### Getting good rounds on interviewing.io

- **Audio quality matters more than you think**. Wired headphones beat AirPods for clarity. The interviewer's first impression is partly your voice quality.
- **Be specific about what you want feedback on**. "I want to work on time management" gets you targeted notes; "general feedback" gets you boilerplate.
- **The recording is the gift**. Re-listen to your own voice the next day. Cringey at first, gold by the third listen.

### The referral track

If your interviewer was strong and the company is one you want, ask: "would you be open to referring me?" Most engineers will if (a) you did well and (b) you ask politely. Don't ask if you bombed; that's awkward for them.

## Pre-mock checklist (15 min before)

- [ ] Wired headphones connected and tested.
- [ ] Quiet room. Door shut. Phone on Do Not Disturb.
- [ ] IDE / coderpad / shared editor open and warmed up.
- [ ] Whiteboard or scratch paper next to you.
- [ ] A glass of water.
- [ ] One specific weakness from the last mock written on a sticky note where you can see it.
- [ ] Last 5 STAR stories visible if it's a behavioural mock.
- [ ] A 5-minute warm-up problem solved (any easy LeetCode) so you're not cold.

---

## During the mock — survival rules

1. **Restate the problem.** Always. Even if it's obvious.
2. **Type your name into the editor first.** Sounds silly; it's a tiny action that breaks first-second freeze.
3. **Keep narrating.** If you go silent for >10 s the interviewer doesn't know if you're thinking, stuck, or distracted. Say "let me think for 30 s" out loud if you need silence.
4. **If you make a mistake, name it and move on.** Don't apologise three times. "I had `<` when I meant `<=` — fixing." That's it.
5. **Time-box yourself.** Look at the clock at minute 15, 30, 40. Adjust.
6. **At minute 35, summarise.** "What I have so far is X. The remaining work is Y." Even if you don't finish, the summary shows control.
7. **Take the feedback in real time.** If they say "what about negative numbers" — don't argue, integrate.

## Post-mock — the 30-minute review

This is where most people fail. They do the mock, feel relieved, and move on. Here's the routine that compounds:

### 0-5 min: capture
- Open your tracking file.
- Score yourself with the [self-grading rubric](05-self-grading-rubric.md).
- Note ONE thing you'd do differently.

### 5-15 min: re-listen (if recorded)
- Skip the parts you remember went well.
- Find the parts you remember as awkward and listen carefully.
- Note specific phrases you used badly. ("Honestly," "kind of," "I think maybe…").

### 15-25 min: re-solve
- Do the problem again, alone, with no time pressure.
- Don't grade yourself on the second solve. The point is to *clear* the residue so you don't carry the failure feeling forward.

### 25-30 min: schedule the next one
- Book the next mock now, while the urgency is fresh.
- 2-3 mocks per week is the sweet spot. More than that, you'll plateau on patterns; fewer and you lose conversational rhythm between sessions.

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it's bad | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I'll only book mocks once I've finished studying" | You will never feel "ready". Mocks expose what to study. | Book your first mock at week 1, not week 8. |
| Doing 5 mocks in a single day before an onsite | Cognitive fatigue makes the last 2 useless and demoralising. | 1-2 the day before, none the day of. |
| Always doing the same problem types | You drill the strengths, never the weaknesses. | Each mock, request a topic from your weakest column. |
| Not recording | Your memory of how the mock went is wildly unreliable. | Use system audio recording (free tools exist). |
| Doing all mocks in the morning when fresh | Real interviews are at all hours. | Schedule at least 3 in your worst time-of-day window. |
| Ghosting partners who give you feedback | Burns the platform's social capital. | One-line thank you, even when feedback was hard. |
| "Mocks don't matter, real interviews are different" | Self-protection move. They are different but the gap is 20%, not 80%. | Treat every mock like a real loop. |

## Two weeks before an onsite — the sprint

| Day | Activity |
|---|---|
| -14 | Pramp mock — coding |
| -13 | Self-review + drill |
| -12 | interviewing.io mock — coding |
| -11 | Self-review + drill |
| -10 | System design mock with a friend |
| -9 | Self-review + read 1 system from `08-system-design/` |
| -8 | Pramp mock — coding |
| -7 | Behavioural with a friend, all stories |
| -6 | Self-review + drill weakest STAR row |
| -5 | interviewing.io mock — system design |
| -4 | Read company blog, 1 hr |
| -3 | One Karat-style paid mock if available |
| -2 | Light review, no new problems |
| -1 | Sleep, no studying |
| 0 | Onsite |

The volume drops on the last 3 days. Cramming hurts more than helps.

## A note on emotion

You will have a mock that goes badly. Possibly several. The candidates who get offers are not the ones who never had a bad mock — they're the ones who:

1. Ran the post-mock review honestly anyway.
2. Booked the next one within 24 hours.
3. Didn't tell themselves the bad mock was a sign they weren't cut out for it.

Two bad mocks in a row → drill the lowest rubric row, don't quit. Three bad mocks in a row → take a 2-day break, come back, run a *fresh* mock without re-reading the failures.
