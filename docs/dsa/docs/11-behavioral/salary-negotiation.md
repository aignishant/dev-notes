# Salary negotiation playbook

> The under-discussed last round. The 30-minute conversation that can move your comp by 20-40%.

<span class="phase-status phase-done">Phase 14 — negotiation playbook</span>

---

## 🏛️ The framing

You've earned the offer. You're now **the company's #1 candidate for this role**. Replacing you costs them weeks. They expect negotiation; not negotiating signals naïveté and leaves money on the table.

Negotiation here is not adversarial — it's information exchange. Stay warm, be specific, be patient.

---

## 🎯 The four levers

| Lever | What it is | Negotiability |
|---|---|---|
| **Base salary** | Annual cash. | Moderate. Bands are tight; ±10% common. |
| **Sign-on bonus** | One-time cash, often year 1 + year 2. | High — recruiters have flex here. |
| **Equity / RSUs** | Stock grant, usually 4-year vest. | High at senior levels, moderate at junior. |
| **Level / tier** | E4 vs E5, L4 vs L5, IC3 vs IC4. | Hardest to move post-offer; biggest lever if it does. |

**The order to push**: Level (if borderline) → Equity → Sign-on → Base. Sign-on is the easiest win and often what closes a deal.

---

## 🤐 Rule 1: don't disclose your number first

This is the biggest single lever in the entire process.

### Why

Whoever names a number first **anchors the conversation**. If you say "I'm hoping for $180k base", they will either:
- Match it (you left money on the table — they were ready to offer $200k).
- Underbid it (you set the ceiling for yourself).

If they name first, you have a starting point to push from.

### How

Recruiters will ask, in many forms:

- "What are your salary expectations?"
- "What's your current comp?"
- "What's your target number?"
- "What range are you looking at?"

For **all** of these, deflect. Templates:

??? note "When asked: 'What are your salary expectations?'"

    > "I'd rather understand the role and the level fit first, then discuss compensation once we both think it's a match. I'm confident we can find a number that works."

??? note "When asked: 'What's your current comp?'"

    In jurisdictions where it's legal to ask (varies — many US states ban this question now):

    > "I'd prefer to focus on what this role is worth, not what I'm currently making. Could you share the range for this level?"

    In jurisdictions where it's banned (CA, NY, CO, WA, MA and many more — check yours), you can simply say:

    > "I understand asking about salary history isn't required by law in [state]. Could we focus on the range for this role?"

??? note "When asked: 'What number would make you sign?'"

    > "I want to evaluate the full package once I see the offer in writing. Could you share what you have in mind, and I'll respond once I've seen it?"

The pattern: **redirect to the role / range / written offer**. Don't lie about your current comp; just decline to share it.

### When you have to give a number

If they will not move forward without one (rare but happens):

- Give a **range**, not a point. "Based on my research, this role at this level pays in the $X-$Y range" where Y is what you actually want and X is the floor.
- Anchor at the **top of public data**. levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Blind, comparably.com.
- Make it the **total comp range**, not base alone.

## 📊 Rule 2: know the market BEFORE you negotiate

You cannot negotiate without numbers.

### Where to look

- **levels.fyi** — best free source for FAANG-tier US/India comp by level. Check the role + level + location combination.
- **Blind (TeamBlind)** — anonymous self-reported. Skewed high; assume the median is real, the top 10% is aspirational.
- **AmbitionBox / Glassdoor** — wider coverage but less recent for top tier.
- **Your network**. One honest number from a friend in the role beats 100 anonymous data points. Worth asking.

### What to gather

For your **target level**, write down:

| | P25 | P50 | P75 | P90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | | | | |
| Equity (annual) | | | | |
| Sign-on (Y1) | | | | |
| **Total Y1** | | | | |

P50 is the median — what they'll likely offer. P75 is what you want. P90 is your stretch.

## 🥊 Rule 3: competing offers — leverage you actually need

Single-offer negotiation is hard; multi-offer negotiation is easy.

### Why competing offers matter

A recruiter cannot escalate to their hiring committee for "I want more" without justification. They CAN escalate for "I have a competing offer at $X". The competing offer gives them air cover to ask for more on your behalf.

### Get one even if your first-choice is far ahead

If you're interviewing and your first-choice is your top pick, **still apply to 2-3 backups** and complete their loops. You don't need to take a competing offer; you need to be **able to truthfully say** "I have a competing offer at $X".

### Don't lie

- Don't fabricate competing offers. Recruiters talk; some companies will ask for the offer letter.
- Don't inflate competing offers. A 20% lie destroys your credibility if caught.
- Don't share the actual offer letter unless you're comfortable with that — but be ready to share level + total comp if asked.

### Use a competing offer well

??? note "Sample script"

    > "Hi [Recruiter], thanks for the offer. I'm genuinely excited about the role. I want to be transparent — I'm also in the final stage with [Company B], and they've extended an offer at [total comp $X], with [breakdown]. [Your company] is my first choice based on the team and product, but the gap is meaningful. Is there room to revisit the offer? I'd love to close this with you."

Note the structure:
1. **Warmth** — "genuinely excited", "first choice".
2. **Specifics** — actual number + breakdown.
3. **Specific ask** — "revisit the offer".
4. **Close intent** — "love to close this".

## 🔄 Rule 4: negotiate components separately

A common mistake is "I want $20k more total". Recruiters need to know **where**.

### Frame asks per-component

> "Could base move from $180k to $195k?"
> "Could the sign-on bonus increase from $20k to $40k?"
> "Could the equity grant be re-evaluated at the next band, given my offer at [Company B]?"

Each component has different flex and different approvers. Asking specifically lets the recruiter route the request correctly.

### Where to push for what

| You want… | Push on… |
|---|---|
| More cash year 1 | Sign-on bonus |
| More cash long-term | Base salary |
| Higher upside | RSU grant |
| Faster vesting | Equity refresh schedule |
| Recognition of prior level | Level / tier |
| Relocation help | Sign-on or relocation bucket (separate budget) |

### Sign-on is the closer

Sign-on is the most flexible lever because it doesn't affect ongoing comp bands or set internal precedents. If you have one stuck request, bias toward sign-on.

## 🏷️ Rule 5: level is the biggest lever, hardest to move

Going from L4 to L5 (or E4 to E5) at FAANG can mean +$50-150k/year in comp depending on company.

### When to push for a level adjustment

- You consistently outperformed the level in interviews (e.g. system design at L5 quality but offered L4).
- A competing offer is at a higher level.
- Your YoE matches the higher level (each company has rough YoE bands).

### How to push

Level changes require a re-review by the hiring committee. Recruiters cannot do this unilaterally. You'll typically need to write a short note (or have your recruiter write it) summarising:

- Examples from your interviews where you demonstrated the higher-level signal.
- Your YoE / scope at current role.
- Competing offer at higher level (if applicable).

The committee will then re-vote. This adds 3-7 days but can be the single biggest move you make.

### Don't push at unrealistic level gaps

L3 asking to be L5 will get rejected. L4 asking to be L5 with strong interview signal can succeed.

## 📜 Rule 6: read the offer letter carefully

Beyond the headline numbers, check:

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Vesting schedule** | 4-year cliff vs 4-year monthly vs accelerated. Big difference at exit. |
| **Cliff** | 1-year cliff means you get 0% if you leave before month 12. |
| **Refresh policy** | Annual refresh grants are often 25-50% of initial — decisive for long-term comp. |
| **Sign-on clawback** | Many sign-ons have a 12-24 month clawback if you leave early. Read terms. |
| **Bonus target + history** | "Target 15%" only matters if actual payouts hit it. Ask about historical payout %. |
| **Notice period** | India offers often 60-90 day notice. Check before accepting. |
| **Bond / service agreement** | Service-co offers may include this; PSU offers always do. |
| **Equity type** | RSUs vs ISOs vs NSOs — tax treatment differs sharply. |
| **Relocation terms** | Lump-sum vs reimbursement; clawback if you leave. |
| **Start date flexibility** | You can usually move it 2-4 weeks. Useful if multiple offers are pending. |

## ⏰ Rule 7: take your time, but don't stall

Most companies give 5-14 days to decide. You can almost always extend by 3-7 days with a polite request.

### When to extend

- Another loop is wrapping up.
- You need to wrap up your current employer's notice or check terms.
- You want a weekend with family before deciding.

### How to extend

??? note "Sample script"

    > "Thanks for the offer. I'd like to give it the careful thought it deserves. Could I have until [date 1 week out] to respond? I'll be in touch sooner if I have questions."

### When NOT to stall

- If you've already received what you asked for and the gap is closed.
- If the recruiter has explicitly said "this is final, please decide".
- Beyond 14 days — companies will start to lose patience and may rescind.

## 🚪 Rule 8: graceful close

### If accepting

??? note "Sample script"

    > "Thanks so much. I'm excited to accept the offer. I'll sign and return today. Looking forward to joining the team on [start date]."

Then send an email confirming the offer terms. Always have it in writing.

### If declining

??? note "Sample script"

    > "Thank you for the offer and the time you and the team invested. After careful thought, I've decided to accept another opportunity. I appreciated the conversations with [names] — they made the decision genuinely difficult. I'd love to stay in touch."

Always decline gracefully. Tech is a small world; recruiters move; teams change. The recruiter you decline today may be your hiring manager in 4 years.

### If the offer fails to move

Sometimes the company genuinely cannot match. Decide if the gap is worth it — equity upside, learning, team, mission can all justify accepting a lower number. **It's OK to accept a lower offer if the non-cash factors line up.** Negotiation is about getting the best offer they can give, not always the highest absolute number.

## 🪤 Common negotiation mistakes

??? warning "Naming a number on the first call"

    The recruiter screen often asks "what are you looking for?" before any technical round. Defer until the offer stage. You don't have leverage yet.

??? warning "Negotiating with the hiring manager, not the recruiter"

    Hiring managers care about closing you, not about comp bands. Recruiters own the comp negotiation. Direct comp asks to the recruiter; team / scope asks to the hiring manager.

??? warning "Threatening to walk"

    "If you can't match $X, I'll have to decline." Sometimes works; often backfires. Soft framing ("the gap is meaningful — is there room to revisit?") preserves the relationship.

??? warning "Negotiating on text / email at high stakes"

    Get on a call for the actual negotiation conversation. Tone matters. Email loses tone.

??? warning "Accepting verbally before seeing written"

    Verbal offers are not binding. Always wait for written before saying "yes". Always sign carefully.

??? warning "Discussing offer details with current coworkers"

    Word travels. Word reaches your manager. Manager may make a counter-offer (good) or may quietly stop trusting you (very bad). Keep it close.

??? warning "The 'exploding offer' trap"

    "You have 24 hours to decide" is a pressure tactic. Push back: "I appreciate the offer; I need 5 days to decide carefully." Most companies will extend; the ones that won't are signalling a culture you may not want.

## 🌍 Region notes

### India

- **Service-co offers** (TCS / Infosys / Wipro / etc.): bands are tight, negotiation is mostly on level + joining bonus, not base. Bond is non-negotiable.
- **Product-co offers** (Flipkart / Razorpay / Swiggy / Zomato): negotiate like FAANG. Equity (ESOPs) is real leverage; understand the strike price and vesting before signing.
- **FAANG India**: same dynamics as US FAANG, scaled. levels.fyi has India numbers.
- **Notice period**: 60-90 days at most companies. Plan offer timing accordingly.
- **CTC vs in-hand**: CTC (cost-to-company) inflates the headline number with employer PF, gratuity, insurance. Always compute in-hand monthly.

### US

- **Salary history bans** (CA, NY, CO, WA, MA, IL, etc.): companies legally cannot require your current salary. Use this.
- **Pay transparency laws**: CA, CO, NY, WA require posted ranges. Use the posted range as your floor.
- **H-1B candidates**: comp negotiation works the same; visa status doesn't reduce your leverage at the offer stage.

## 🎓 Special cases

### New grad / first job

You have less leverage but it's not zero.

- **Multiple offers** still work. Compete them.
- **Sign-on bonus** is the easiest ask — recruiters often have a $5-15k flex bucket for new grads.
- **Don't fight on level** — entry-level bands are inflexible.
- **Read the bond / clawback terms** carefully.

### Internal transfer / promotion

- Your manager / HR business partner is the negotiator, not a recruiter.
- Comparable external offer is the strongest leverage.
- Refresh equity grants are the typical instrument, not base.

### Switching from product to service or vice-versa

- Don't anchor on your current band. Service-co levels don't translate cleanly to product-co levels.
- Get peer ranges at the target company first; negotiate against those, not against your current comp.

## ✅ The pre-negotiation checklist

Before the comp call:

- [ ] I have data on P50/P75 for this role at this level at this company.
- [ ] I have at least one competing offer (or strong interest from another company).
- [ ] I have decided which lever (level / equity / sign-on / base) I'm pushing hardest on.
- [ ] I have my walk-away number — the offer below which I genuinely won't accept.
- [ ] I have my target number — what I want, framed as a stretch I can defend.
- [ ] I will not name a number first.
- [ ] I will get the final offer in writing before agreeing.

## ➡️ Practice script

Practice the full conversation aloud, with a friend playing the recruiter:

1. Recruiter: "What are your salary expectations?"
   You: [deflect]

2. Recruiter: "We're prepared to offer $X total."
   You: [thank, ask for time, plan ask]

3. Recruiter call 2:
   You: [present competing offer + specific ask per component]

4. Recruiter: "Let me see what I can do."
   You: [thank, set follow-up time]

5. Recruiter call 3 with revised offer.
   You: [accept / counter once more / decline]

The conversation is short, repeated, and surprisingly mechanical once you've done it once. The first time is the hardest.
